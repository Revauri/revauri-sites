Here's the question as it usually gets asked: "We're a five-person shop. The phones, the follow-ups, the review asks — it's too much. Do I hire a person, or is this AI employee thing actually a job?"

The honest answer is that a lot of shops should hire a person. Not yet for the AI version — or not for *this* job. Anyone who tells you otherwise without asking what the work is, how often it happens, and what has to stay your call is selling.

But "not yet" is doing real work in that sentence. The threshold is not whether you like the demo. It is whether the job is repeatable weekly work that can leave your plate, or judgment that should stay on it. Below that line, a person is the right hire. Above it, keeping a person on a job an AI employee can run is how you pay salary for coordination you no longer needed.

We still will not put a number on this page. [We quote the job](/pricing). What you can do here is run the comparison with *your* hours.

## What a person is actually being hired to do

Worth being precise, because "I need help" is not a role.

Most local service owners who start looking are not hiring a technician. They are hiring someone for the work around the work: answering after hours, catching overflow, texting the quote that sat for four days, asking for the review, reminding tomorrow's jobs, cleaning the inbox. That person is rarely full-time on one of those jobs. They are part-time across five of them, plus the things that come up.

A good person on that seat does three things you cannot fake with a tool:

**They take a messy call and make a judgment.** The job is not in the script. The customer is upset. Someone has to decide whether to hold a slot or send it to you.

**They are a body on site, or a voice a customer already trusts.** There is no workflow for "go look at the leak." There is no workflow for "Mrs. Chen only talks to you."

**They absorb the leftover.** The thing you did not think to name when you wrote the job post.

If what you actually need is those three, hire the person. An AI employee will not become them if you squint. We will tell you that on the call, because a bad hire in the other direction wastes a month you do not get back.

If what you actually need is the repeatable slice — the same five follow-ups, the same after-hours capture, the same review ask, every week — then the person you were about to hire was going to spend most of their hours on work that does not need a body. That is the comparison.

## Where an AI employee holds

This is the part that is true often enough to write down, and still not true for every shop.

**The work already has a shape.** Missed calls. Quotes that went quiet. Past customers who have not been asked back. Review requests after a finished job. Inbox busywork that is the same three folders every Monday. You can name it in a sentence. If you cannot name it, you are not ready to hand it off — you are still discovering the job, and a person in the room will discover it faster.

**It happens every week.** Not twice a year. Not "when we remember." Cadence is what makes a hire cheaper than a heroics sprint. A workflow you run once is a project. A workflow you run every week is a job.

**The output can be checked, then trusted.** During setup you approve so it learns your voice. Texts sound like you. The callback hold is a time you would actually honor. After that stretch, you should not be living in the tool. If you cannot imagine a week where you only read the note, the job is still too judgment-heavy.

**The tools you already use can receive the work.** The phone system, the inbox, the scheduler, the place reviews live. An AI employee built around a brand-new stack is a migration, not a hire. We build around how you already run. That is not a slogan. It is the only way the work actually leaves.

Those four are the same bar we use on [what a standard hire includes](/pricing). Two workflows, named in writing. We run them. You get the note.

## Where it does not

Say this plainly, because most writing in this category pretends the list is empty.

**Anything that spends your reputation in a way only you should.** Pricing a messy job. Promising Saturday when Saturday is already full. Telling a customer the last crew's work was fine when you have not seen it. Those stay yours.

**Work that is different every time.** A one-off insurance fight. A permitting maze. A customer who needs a long conversation before they will let anyone on the property. A person handles that. A workflow will flatten it into the wrong template.

**Work you have not decided how *you* do yet.** If your follow-up is "we should text them sometime," there is nothing to run. Write the job first — even badly — or hire a person to sit in it with you until it has a shape. Then the AI employee has something to take.

**A shop that wanted a manager.** If the painful job is "someone to own the crew, the schedule, and the customer when I am on a job," that is a person. Do not buy a workflow and hope it becomes a GM.

## You still approve during setup

This is the part people skip, then blame the tool.

The first stretch is not "set and forget." You see the messages before they sound like you. You correct the ones that are too stiff, too salesy, too fast to offer a time you cannot keep. That is the job teaching the hire your voice. It is also you deciding what must never go out without you.

Then it runs. The weekly note is how you stay in the picture without doing the picture. What went out. What is waiting. The odd case.

If a shop cannot describe that handoff — approve, then run — they are selling you either a toy you will babysit or a black box you will not trust. Both fail the "worth it" test, because the hours do not come back.

## How to run the comparison with your hours

Skip the industry averages. Your shop is not an average.

1. **Name the job in one sentence.** After-hours missed calls. Quotes that sit. Review asks. Pick the painful one, not all of them. Two workflows is a hire. Twelve is a fantasy.

2. **Count a real week.** How many times did that job show up. How long did it take you, or the person you were about to hire, including the "I'll do it tonight" hours that never hit a timesheet.

3. **Mark what has to stay yours.** If more than half the time is judgment, hire the person. If most of the time is the same motion with a small exception pile, the AI employee is in range.

4. **Add the hours you would spend *managing* a person in that seat.** Hiring, showing them the phone, rewriting their texts, covering their days off. That cost is real and almost never in the "just get a part-timer" math.

5. **Ask what week six looks like.** For a person: they are on the job, or they called out, or they are still learning your voice. For an AI employee: the note, the exceptions, the rest of the week on the work you actually bill.

Then the only question is whether the hours that leave are the ones that were burning you. If the painful hours are the judgments, the comparison will tell you to hire a person. If the painful hours are the repeats, keeping a person on them is how shops stay tired and still feel understaffed.

## Three situations, three answers

**Hire the person if:** the job is messy, physical, or tied to a relationship only a human can hold; you do not yet know how you want the work done; or you wanted a manager, not a workflow. Revisit the AI hire when the repeats are obvious.

**The AI employee is probably the better hire if:** you can name two weekly jobs, you are the one doing them after dinner, and what you wanted back was time on the tools and time off the clock — not another person to check on.

**It depends if:** you are somewhere between. In that case pick the one job that hurts most and run the week count on that job only. Do not commission a whole "AI transformation" to solve a review ask.

## If you are not ready, do these four things

Genuinely useful whether you hire us, hire a person, or wait.

1. **Write the two jobs down.** The sentence you would put in a job post. If you cannot, you are not buying yet.
2. **Track one week of those jobs.** Tally marks on a pad are enough. You cannot compare hours you will not count.
3. **Decide what you will never let go out without you.** That list is the hire's fence. It is also how you sleep.
4. **Look at who would run it in week six.** You, a new person, or someone whose name you already know. That is the offer. Everything else is packaging.

## The short version

An AI employee is worth it when the painful job is repeatable, weekly, and able to leave your plate after you approve the voice. A person is worth it when the painful job is judgment, presence, or a role you have not named yet. Plenty of good shops are honestly in that second group.

The mistake is not hiring a person. It is hiring a person for the repeats, or hiring an AI employee for the judgments, because nobody counted a week.
