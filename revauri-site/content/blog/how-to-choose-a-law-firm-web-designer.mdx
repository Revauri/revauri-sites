A firm we spoke with picked their web designer the way most firms do. The mockup looked sharp, the salesperson was likeable, the price was in range. Eleven months later they wanted to add a practice area. They were told it would be a change request, billed hourly, scheduled for the following month. When they asked to move the site somewhere else, they learned the design was licensed to them, not owned by them, and the content lived in a proprietary system that didn't export.

Nothing illegal happened. Nothing was even hidden — it was all in the agreement they signed. They just never asked the questions that would have surfaced it.

Choosing a web designer is not really a design decision. Almost everyone showing you work can produce something that looks good in a mockup. What separates them is structural: who does the work, what you own at the end, and what happens the day after launch. Those things are knowable up front, and almost nobody asks.

## What actually goes wrong

Not "bad design." Design is the part everyone gets approximately right. The failures cluster elsewhere.

**You can't leave.** The most common and most expensive problem. Your site is on a proprietary platform, your content doesn't export cleanly, and the design is licensed rather than owned. Your monthly fee is no longer buying maintenance — it's buying continued access to your own website. This is not an accident; it's a retention strategy.

**Nobody is accountable for whether it works.** The site launches, everyone congratulates each other, and no one has defined what success was supposed to look like. Twelve months later you have no idea whether it's producing cases, because nobody agreed in advance on what to measure.

**The person who impressed you isn't the person building it.** You met a salesperson. Design goes to a subcontractor, development to another, and your feedback travels through an account manager who is relaying messages between people who never speak. Every round trip loses something.

**SEO was promised but never scoped.** "SEO included" appears in the proposal. What that means is never specified, so it becomes whatever is cheapest to claim afterward.

**Speed was nobody's job.** This matters more for personal injury than almost any other practice area, and I'll come back to it.

## The questions that actually separate designers

Ask these. The answers sort candidates faster than any portfolio review.

### "Who owns the site, the design, and the content if I stop paying you?"

Ask it exactly that bluntly, and get the answer in writing in the agreement — not in an email, not verbally on a call.

**A good answer** is specific and unhesitating: you own the design and the content, here's how you'd take it with you, here's what you'd need (a hosting account, the code, a database export). It might come with an honest caveat — you'd need someone technical to host it — and that's fine. Honest specifics are the signal.

**A bad answer** is vague or reframes the question. "You'll never want to leave." "Everything's included in your plan." "It's all managed for you." Those are answers about how leaving is unlikely, not about what happens if you do.

This question is uncomfortable for a large share of the market, which is exactly why it's useful. Vendors whose business model depends on switching costs cannot answer it cleanly.

### "Can I see two live sites you built, not mockups?"

Not portfolio screenshots. Not renders. URLs you can open, click through, and test on your own phone. Then actually do it.

A designer who is genuinely building custom, fast sites will hand you a list without hesitating. Someone reselling a template will steer you back to images. If the only evidence of quality is a picture of the homepage, you're evaluating a picture. We wrote about this failure mode in more detail in [our breakdown of why law firm website quotes vary so wildly](/blog/law-firm-website-cost) — the render is the easiest part of the job to make impressive and the least predictive of the result.

### "Who, specifically, is doing the work?"

You want names and roles, and you want to know how many handoffs sit between your feedback and the person who acts on it.

Neither answer is automatically wrong. A team of eight can be excellent. One person can be excellent. But the structure tells you what you're paying for and how fast changes will happen. If the answer is fuzzy — "our team handles that" — you're likely funding coordination overhead rather than build time.

### "What happens after launch, and what does month thirteen look like?"

Every credible arrangement includes something ongoing: hosting, SSL, backups, updates, security patches. That's normal and you should want it.

What you're testing is whether the terms are clear. What's included? What counts as a billable change versus routine maintenance? What's the notice period to cancel? What happens to the site if you do? A quote that ends at launch and goes quiet about the following year is not a complete quote.

### "How will we know in six months whether this worked?"

The best answer involves numbers agreed before the build starts — signed cases from the site, form submissions, calls from mobile, whatever maps to how your firm actually intakes work.

The worst answer is "it'll look a lot better." A firm that can't tell you what it's optimizing for is going to optimize for whatever's easiest to show you at the end, which is usually aesthetics.

## What's specific about personal injury

Most advice about hiring a web designer is generic. Two things are not, for a PI firm.

**Your website is an intake tool, not a brochure.** Someone who was rear-ended two hours ago is on their phone, possibly in a parking lot, possibly on a bad connection, possibly in pain, and they are going to contact one of the first firms whose site loads and makes it obvious how to reach a human. Everything downstream of that — your case results, your credentials, your brand — only matters if the page loads and the phone number is a single tap.

So ask specifically: how fast will this be on a phone on mobile data, and what happens when somebody wants to talk to a person at 2 a.m.? If the answer is a contact form that emails an inbox nobody reads until Monday, that's a gap. After-hours triage, click-to-call, and a genuinely fast mobile page are worth more to a PI firm than another round of design polish.

This is the part of [the Lion Law build](/portfolio/lion-law) that mattered most. Seventeen hand-built pages with no framework and no build step, so the pages load fast, plus a chatbot trained on the firm's real intake questions handling first contact around the clock. The gold-on-black brand system is what people notice first. The intake path is what actually does the work.

**Trust has to be established before anyone reads a word.** PI is a crowded, high-stakes category where a visitor is cross-shopping three firms in one sitting. If your site resembles a template they saw on the last two firms' sites, you've lost a differentiator you paid for.

## A twenty-minute check you can run yourself

Before any call, before any proposal:

1. **Run their own site through Google's PageSpeed Insights.** A designer who sells performance and whose own site is slow has told you something. This takes ninety seconds and is remarkably clarifying.
2. **Open two of their client sites on your phone**, on cellular, not office wifi. Time how long until you can tap a phone number.
3. **Look for the same layout twice** across their portfolio. Shared structure with different colors and photos means you're buying a template, which may be fine — but you should know, and you shouldn't pay custom rates for it.
4. **Call the number on a client's site.** See what happens. You're evaluating the intake path you'd be buying.
5. **Search the client firm's name plus their city.** If sites they built years ago don't rank for their own brand, treat "SEO included" with skepticism.

None of this requires technical knowledge. All of it is more predictive than a portfolio review.

## When you should not hire a small studio

Worth saying plainly, because most articles like this are written by the people they're recommending.

**Hire a larger agency when** you have multiple offices across states, you need integration with a case management system, you want a dedicated account manager and a team on call, or you're running significant paid media and want the site and ad operations under one roof. Those are real needs and a solo operator or small studio is the wrong shape for them.

**Buy a template when** you're a new solo practice with no case volume yet and no budget to spare. A clean template site now beats a custom site you can't afford. Revisit it once you know what's actually converting.

**A small studio or independent designer makes sense when** you want genuinely custom work without funding an agency's overhead, you'd rather talk directly to the person building it, and you value being able to get a small change made this week instead of next month.

The honest version is that there's no universally correct answer — only a correct answer for the size and stage of your firm. Anyone who tells you their model is right for everybody is selling, not advising.

## The short version

Design quality is table stakes and nearly everyone clears it. What varies is ownership, accountability, structure, and what year two looks like. Ask who owns it, ask who's building it, ask for live URLs, ask what happens after launch, and agree on what success means before anyone writes code.
