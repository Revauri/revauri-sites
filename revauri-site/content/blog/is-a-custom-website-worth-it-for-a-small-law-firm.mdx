Here's the question as it usually gets asked: "We're a two-attorney firm. There's a template that looks fine for a few hundred dollars. Do we really need to spend thousands on something custom?"

The honest answer is that a lot of firms don't. Not yet. And anyone who tells you otherwise without asking about your caseload is selling.

But "not yet" is doing real work in that sentence, because the threshold is specific and it's not about how much you like the design. It's about whether your website is a business card or an intake channel. Below that line, a template is genuinely the right call. Above it, staying on one starts costing more than replacing it would.

## What templates actually do well

Worth being fair about this, because the case for custom is weaker if you have to exaggerate.

A modern template on a mainstream platform gives you a mobile-responsive site, hosting and SSL handled, a working contact form, reasonable typography, and a security posture that's someone else's job. It goes live in days rather than weeks. For a new practice, that is a completely defensible decision — a clean template site now beats a custom site you can't afford, and beats the site you keep meaning to commission and never do.

If you're pre-revenue, or your cases come entirely from referrals and you just need somewhere for people to confirm you exist, stop reading and buy a template. You'll know when that stops being true.

## The five places templates break for PI firms

These are specific to personal injury, and they're where the calculus changes.

**Practice-area depth.** A template handles one page per practice area. It handles poorly the situation where auto accidents, premises liability, workers' comp, and medical malpractice each need genuinely different page structures because the client's questions, the evidence involved, and the search intent all differ. You end up flattening four different conversations into one layout because that's what the theme supports. Meanwhile the firm across town has a page built specifically around "rideshare accident" and it ranks.

**The speed ceiling.** Templates get slow in a predictable way: the base theme is acceptable, then you add a slider plugin, a form plugin, a chat widget, an SEO plugin, a review widget, and each one loads its own scripts. Nobody makes a bad decision — the accumulation is the problem. There's a floor you can't get under, because the loading behavior isn't yours to control. For a practice whose visitors arrive on cellular, often distressed, often on older phones, that ceiling is a conversion tax you pay every day.

**Intake is generic by default.** A template's contact form emails an inbox. What a PI firm actually needs is closer to triage: capturing whether there's a police report, whether they've been treated, whether they've spoken to an insurer — and doing something useful at 11pm on a Saturday when a meaningful share of accident-related searching happens. Templates support a form. They don't support your intake process.

**You look like the other firms who bought the same theme.** In most practice areas, resemblance is harmless. In personal injury, your visitor is cross-shopping three firms in a single sitting, deciding largely on trust signals, in a category where they already suspect everyone is interchangeable. Looking like a template confirms the thing you most need to disprove.

**Portability.** Some template platforms make it genuinely hard to leave — content that doesn't export cleanly, designs licensed rather than owned. This is worth checking before you commit either way; we went through the specific questions to ask in [how to choose a web designer for a personal injury law firm](/blog/how-to-choose-a-law-firm-web-designer).

## The break-even math, with your numbers not mine

This is the part most articles skip, usually by quoting an industry-average case value. I'm not going to, because average case value across personal injury is close to meaningless — a soft-tissue settlement and a catastrophic-injury verdict aren't the same business.

Use your own figures instead:

1. **Your average fee per signed case.** You know this number.
2. **How many signed cases came from your website last year.** If you can't answer this, that's the actual finding — and it's worth fixing before you spend anything, because you're about to make an investment decision blind.
3. **The all-in cost of the build**, plus twelve months of whatever ongoing plan comes with it. Not the build price alone. (What that should look like is covered in [what a personal injury law firm website should cost](/blog/law-firm-website-cost).)

Then the only question is how many additional signed cases over a two-to-three-year life would cover it.

For most firms doing this honestly, the answer comes out surprisingly small — often a fraction of a single case, because PI fees are large relative to web development costs. Which is why the real question usually isn't "can this pay for itself." It's **"is my website actually a channel that produces cases at all, or just a brochure?"** If it produces none today and you have no intake path, a prettier site may not change that. If it produces some, improving the path they travel is one of the highest-leverage things available to you.

## The part nobody prices in: what switching later costs

If you choose a template now and outgrow it in two years, you don't simply pay for the custom site then. You pay for the move as well, and it's worth knowing what's in that bill before you decide.

**Content re-entry.** Every practice-area page, attorney bio, case result, and blog post has to come across. If the platform exports cleanly, this is tedious. If it doesn't, it's manual retyping, and it's billable.

**URL changes and the rankings attached to them.** This is the expensive one. Template platforms often impose their own URL structure — `/pages/auto-accidents` rather than `/auto-accidents`, or dated blog paths. Change those without mapping every old URL to its new one with a permanent redirect and you throw away whatever ranking authority those pages accumulated. Firms discover this the month after launch, when traffic drops and nobody can explain why.

**Anything wired into the old platform.** Forms feeding a CRM, review widgets, chat, tracking and conversion setup. Each one has to be rebuilt and re-tested.

None of this makes a template the wrong choice today. It just means the honest comparison isn't *template price* versus *custom price* — it's template price plus a future migration versus doing it once. For a firm genuinely early in its life, paying twice is still the right call, because the cheap option now is what lets you exist at all. For a firm already generating cases from search, paying twice is just paying twice.

Two things reduce that future bill regardless of what you choose now: keep your URL structure clean and shallow from day one, and confirm before you commit that your content can be exported in a usable format.

## Three situations, three answers

**Stay on the template if:** your cases come from referrals and reputation, you're in your first year or two, your website has produced no measurable intake, and you don't yet have the case volume to justify the spend. Revisit in twelve months.

**Custom is probably overdue if:** you're competing for the same local searches as two or three other firms, you're spending money on ads pointing at pages you can't restructure, you have multiple practice areas that deserve real depth, or your site is slow on a phone and you've hit the ceiling of what plugins can fix. Every month at that point is measurable lost intake.

**It depends if:** you're somewhere between. In that case pick the one thing your site most needs — usually mobile speed or the intake path — and fix that specifically rather than commissioning a whole rebuild to solve one problem.

## If you're not ready for custom, do these four things

Genuinely useful regardless of who you hire, or whether you hire anyone:

1. **Run your site through Google's PageSpeed Insights on mobile.** If it's slow, audit your plugins before you audit your design. Removing three you don't use often beats a redesign.
2. **Make your phone number a tap-to-call link** on every page, above the fold on mobile. This is a small change with an outsized effect for PI, and a surprising number of firm sites still don't do it.
3. **Give your top practice area a real page** — not three paragraphs and a stock photo. What to expect, what it costs the client, how long it takes, what you need from them. This is content work, not design work, and you can do it yourself.
4. **Decide what you're measuring**, and start. Calls, forms, whichever. You cannot evaluate next year's investment without this year's baseline.

## The short version

A custom site is worth it when your website is an intake channel and the template's ceiling is costing you cases. It is not worth it when your site is a business card and your cases come from elsewhere — and plenty of good firms are honestly in that second group.

The mistake isn't choosing a template. It's staying on one for three years after you outgrew it, because nobody was measuring.
