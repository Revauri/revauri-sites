A managing partner at a two-attorney PI firm called around to four web design shops last month. The quotes came back at $2,500, $9,000, $18,000, and "let's hop on a call to discuss investment levels." Same firm. Same scope, roughly. Four wildly different numbers, and nothing in any of the pitches explained why.

That gap isn't a scam, and it's not even always about quality. Some of it is genuinely different scope — a template versus a bespoke build. But a lot of it is something most quotes never say out loud: what you're paying for isn't just the website, it's whoever and whatever stands between you and the person actually building it. Here's what actually separates the numbers, so you can tell what you're being quoted before you're the one asking why your bill doesn't match the guy down the hall's.

## The four real tiers

### $1,500–$4,000: Template, filled in

This is a pre-built theme — the same one available to any of the thousand other firms who bought that template — with your logo, your attorney bios, and your practice areas dropped into the existing layout. The structure, the page flow, and usually the stock photography are not yours; they're licensed.

What you get: a working, mobile-friendly site, live in one to three weeks, usually on WordPress with a page builder plugin.

What you don't get: any real say in layout, a design that differentiates you from the three other firms who bought the same theme, or a site built with your specific practice areas' search intent in mind. If your city has one dominant PI firm and you're trying to look like a credible alternative, a visitor who's cross-shopping will notice the resemblance to a dozen other lawyer sites.

This tier is a legitimate choice for a firm that needs *a* website this month and doesn't yet have the budget or case volume to justify more. It is not the right choice for a firm competing hard for paid leads or organic rankings in a contested market.

(You'll notice this range sits right up against the next tier below, with little daylight between them — that's real, not sloppy math. The split isn't a hard price line, it's what you're actually buying at that price: a licensed theme versus a designer's original layout work.)

### $4,000–$12,000: Semi-custom

A real designer works from your brand — your colors, your fonts, your photography if you have it — inside a flexible template framework rather than a fixed theme. You get custom layout decisions on your key pages (homepage, practice areas, case results) even if the underlying CMS and component library are shared infrastructure.

This is where most solo and small PI firms land, and for good reason: it's the first tier where the site actually looks and reads like *your* firm instead of a reskinned template, while staying fast to build (typically three to six weeks) and reasonable to maintain.

The tradeoff is ceiling, not floor. You can get a genuinely good site here. You can't get a fully bespoke information architecture built around your specific caseload, your specific local competitors, or an AI chatbot wired into your intake process — those live in the next tier up.

### $12,000–$20,000+: Fully custom

No template, no shared component library. Every page is designed from a blank canvas around your firm specifically — your case results, your specific practice mix (auto accidents vs. medical malpractice vs. workers' comp changes what a homepage needs to say), and your local competitive landscape.

This tier is also where you start seeing functionality that a template can't cleanly support: an AI chatbot trained on your firm's actual FAQs and intake questions for after-hours triage, a case-results page with real filtering, a practice-area carousel that doesn't fight the CMS to update, and technical SEO built into the page structure from day one rather than bolted on with a plugin afterward.

Is this tier worth it for every firm? No. If you're a solo practitioner three months into practice, it's overkill — go semi-custom and revisit this once you have case volume and a clearer sense of what's actually converting. If you're an established firm competing against three other PI shops in your metro for the same "car accident lawyer near me" searches, this level of build is usually where the math works, because the site itself becomes part of your competitive differentiation instead of a cost center. But — and this is the part most cost guides skip — the $12,000–$20,000 price tag above isn't actually the cost of *that work*. It's the cost of that work plus everything wrapped around it at a typical agency. More on that below.

### $20,000+: Enterprise / multi-location

Multiple offices, multiple states, integrations with your case management system, dedicated landing pages per practice area per city. Most solo and small firms will never need this tier, and if someone quotes you here for a single-location practice, ask what specifically is driving the number — it should be a real answer about scope, not a markup on the same semi-custom build.

## What the $12,000–$20,000+ tier is actually paying for

At a traditional agency, that number is rarely just design and development time. It's design and development time plus everything the agency has built up around it: a salesperson who ran your discovery call, an account manager who relays messages between you and whoever's actually building the site, a project manager keeping the internal timeline on track, office overhead, and often a design phase and a build phase handled by two different people (or two different vendors) who have to hand work back and forth. Many of these firms are also still building on WordPress, which means a "custom" theme is frequently a heavily modified off-the-shelf one, and a genuinely custom build can stretch to three or four months once it passes through that many hands.

None of that is a scam, either. Those layers exist because a firm with a sales team and account managers has real payroll to cover, and for a certain kind of client — one who wants a dedicated account rep and a large team on call — that structure is worth paying for. The point isn't that agencies do bad work. It's that a chunk of a $15,000 quote is funding the coordination required to get a project through a multi-person, multi-department pipeline, not the hours spent actually designing and building your pages.

## Price tracks overhead, not quality

Here's the thing that gets lost in most "how much should a website cost" posts: the tier tells you what's being built. The price tells you who's building it and how much structure sits between you and them.

A solo operator or small studio doing design and development themselves, on a modern stack, doesn't carry a sales team, an account-management layer, or a multi-month WordPress build process. There's one person (or a very small team) making every decision, and a framework like Next.js paired with Tailwind and deployed on Vercel makes a bespoke layout fast to build rather than expensive to build — the tooling that used to make "custom" slow and costly has largely closed that gap. That's a structurally different cost basis, not a shortcut, and it's why genuinely custom work can exist well outside the $12,000–$20,000 agency range without being a lesser version of it.

Nothing about it is a theme with the logo swapped in, and none of it required a sales team or a project manager to produce. (We're citing this as proof that this kind of build exists outside agency pricing, not disclosing what the client paid — that stays between us and the firm.)

The practical takeaway: when you're comparing quotes, ask what the tier gets you *and* who's actually building it. A $4,000 difference between two quotes for the same scope is often just overhead. A $4,000 difference for a fundamentally different tier of work — template versus bespoke — is the real thing you should be evaluating.

## What actually moves the price, beyond the tier

The tier is a starting point. Within any tier, a few specific things move the number:

**Page count.** A homepage plus five practice-area pages plus attorney bios plus a case-results page plus a blog is a very different scope than a homepage and a contact form. Get a page list before you get a price — "the website" isn't a scope, a page count is.

**Content readiness.** If you're handing over finished copy, real case results, and professional photography, the build moves faster and cheaper. If the designer has to write your copy, source stock photography, and chase you for case details, that's billable work even if it's not itemized separately — it just shows up as a longer timeline or a higher number.

**SEO scope.** A site with clean URLs, proper heading structure, and fast load times is genuinely different engineering work than a site with those things bolted on after launch. If SEO isn't mentioned in your quote at all, ask directly what's included — "we'll optimize it" is not a scope, it's a sentence.

**Chatbot or intake automation.** A chatbot trained on your firm's real FAQs and wired into your actual intake workflow is a meaningfully different build than an embedded generic widget. Ask specifically what it's trained on and where the conversation goes when someone actually wants to talk to a person.

**Ongoing maintenance.** Almost every credible quote today includes some form of monthly plan for hosting, SSL, backups, and updates — commonly somewhere in the $50–$500+/month range depending on what's covered. Get this in writing as part of the quote, not as a surprise after launch. A one-time build price with no mention of what happens the day after launch is missing half the real cost of ownership.

## The render is not the site

Almost every quote comes with a mockup — a static image of what the homepage will look like. That's normal and useful. What's not normal is when the mockup is the only thing you've seen before you sign. A polished homepage render tells you nothing about whether the practice-area pages will be built with the same care, whether the site will actually load fast on a phone in a parking lot after an accident, or whether the "custom" layout is the same template you saw on two other firms' sites last week with different photos dropped in.

Before you commit at any tier, ask to see the designer's other live sites — not renders, not portfolio screenshots, actual URLs you can open and click through. Run one through Google's PageSpeed Insights yourself. A firm that's genuinely building custom, fast sites won't hesitate to hand you a list of live examples. A firm reselling a theme usually will.

## What to ask before you sign anything

Whatever tier you're shopping in, four questions will tell you more than any price sheet:

1. **Can I see the page list, not just "a website"?** If the answer is vague, the price will be too.
2. **Who's writing the copy, and is that included?** Copy is often the single biggest hidden cost in a "cheap" quote.
3. **What happens to my rankings and content if I cancel the maintenance plan?** Some agencies build sites that quietly stop working — or stop being yours — the day you stop paying them monthly.
4. **Can I see a live site you built at this exact tier?** Not a mockup. A live, indexed, real-client site. If they can't show you one, they haven't built one.

Go back to that managing partner's four quotes. The tier tells you what's being built — template, semi-custom, fully custom, enterprise. The price tells you who's building it and how much overhead you're funding to get there. Once you can separate those two questions, four wildly different numbers stop being confusing and start being useful information.
